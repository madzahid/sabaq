Product Brief · Draft 1

Quran for Hifz

سبق

A Quran reader built for memorisation — and for the person sitting across from the child, listening.

Prepared for Zahid · 14 August 2026

Every hifz app on the market is a reader with a bookmark. None of them serve the moment that actually decides whether a child memorises the Quran: a student reciting from memory while someone listens, catches the slips, and feeds the prompt. Sabaq is built around that moment.

01 — The problem

Memorisation happens out loud, but the tools are silent

A child memorising the Quran works from a fixed printed page — in South Asia, the 16-line Indo-Pak Mushaf, often with colour-coded tajwid. The page layout is not decoration; it is the memory itself. A hafiz recalls that a verse sits third line from the top on the left-hand page. Any app that reflows the text destroys the thing being memorised.

The PDF collections circulating online — such as the juz-by-juz 16-line colour tajwid set Zahid's son currently uses — solve authenticity and layout, and nothing else. They cannot hide a line so a child can test recall. They cannot loop an ayah. They cannot record where he stumbled last Tuesday. And they offer nothing at all to the parent or teacher listening to him recite.

The insight

The listener is the missing user. Every hifz product builds for the student alone, but memorisation is verified by a second person — a teacher, a parent, a study partner — holding the Mushaf and marking mistakes in pencil. Digitise that and you own a workflow nobody has touched.

02 — Who it is for

Three people, one session

The student

A child or adult memorising, typically 5–20 lines a day, revising far more than that. Needs the exact page they know, offline, plus a way to self-test.

The listener

A parent, ustadh, or peer following along while the student recites. Hands and eyes are busy. Needs to mark a slip in one tap without breaking the child's flow.

The teacher over time

The same listener, weeks later, deciding what to revise. Needs to see which pages keep bleeding and which are solid — evidence, not memory.

Reach

Web, Android, iOS, iPad from a single codebase. Full offline operation is non-negotiable — much of the audience studies without reliable data.

03 — Feature one

A Mushaf that is pixel-faithful to the printed page

Rendered from a glyph font plus page-and-line layout data, not from PDFs and not from reflowable text. This is the approach Quran.com uses. It gives an exact reproduction of the 16-line page that stays crisp at any zoom, weighs a fraction of page images, and — critically — makes every individual word a tappable element that can be highlighted, blurred, or synced to audio.

  • Blur & reveal. Hide a line, an ayah, or the whole page; recall it; tap to check. This single feature is the reason a student opens the app alone.
  • Tajwid colouring preserved from the printed edition, with a tap-a-letter legend explaining which rule applies.
  • Loop a range — repeat ayahs 5 to 8 twenty times with a chosen qari, with the current word highlighted as he recites.
  • Two-page spread on iPad, matching how the physical Mushaf is actually read.
  • Fully offline. Fonts and layout data ship inside the app; audio caches on demand.

04 — Feature two

Listening mode — marking mistakes as the child recites

The student recites from memory. The listener holds the device open at the same page and taps a word the instant something goes wrong. That is the entire interaction.

Design constraint

The listener is looking at the page and concentrating on the child's voice. No dialogs, no menus, no confirmations. One tap on the word, and the mark is committed. A small chip fades in for three seconds offering to reclassify the mark — ignore it and it saves as the default.

The error taxonomy

Teachers do not count generic "mistakes" — different slips carry different meaning, and the app should record them separately:

MarkWhat it means, and why it matters
اٹکنا · HesitationPaused, then recovered unaided. Knowledge is present but fragile.
لقمہ · Prompt givenNeeded to be told the word. This is the metric madrasas already grade by — luqmas per page. Make it the one-tap default.
SubstitutionSaid a different word than the text.
Omission / additionSkipped or inserted a word or ayah.
متشابہات · Parallel jumpSlid into a near-identical verse elsewhere in the Quran. The single largest cause of hifz breakdown.
Tajwid slipMadd length, ghunnah, makhraj. Counted separately — a pronunciation fault is not a memory fault, and conflating them punishes good memorisation.

Record the session automatically

Audio records for the whole recitation, and every mark becomes a timestamp into that recording. Afterwards the student taps a mark and hears the exact moment he slipped, in his own voice. Cheap to build, and nothing else teaches self-correction as fast. It also lets a parent review a session they were not present for.

Catch hesitations without speech recognition

True recitation-checking ASR is expensive and hard. It is also unnecessary at first: detect silences longer than about two seconds and surface them as suggested hesitations for the listener to confirm or dismiss after the session. That catches most stumbles with basic audio analysis. Real ASR belongs in version three, if ever.

The mutashabihat advantage

Every pair of near-identical ayahs in the Quran can be computed in advance from the text. So when a student jumps from one verse into its twin, Sabaq does not merely log an error — it shows both verses side by side with the differing words highlighted and adds the pair to his revision queue. No paper system can do this. It is the feature people would switch for.

05 — Feature three

What the sessions add up to

Each session saves as a record: student, date, type (سبق new lesson / سبقی recent revision / منزل old revision), the range recited, every mark with its timestamp, the audio, and a grade auto-suggested from the luqma count that the listener can override.

Across weeks this becomes a weakness heat map laid over the actual Mushaf pages. A teacher sees at a glance which pages of a juz keep failing, and the revision queue prioritises them — instead of the usual blind cycling that lets old memorisation quietly rot while new lessons pile on top.

07 — How it gets built

One codebase, four platforms

React + TypeScript, built with Vite, wrapped in Capacitor. Written once, shipped as a progressive web app plus genuine App Store and Play Store builds. Capacitor rather than React Native because this app is fundamentally a beautifully typeset document reader — the web rendering engine is exactly the right tool for the job, and iPad gets its two-page spread almost for free.

Local-first data in SQLite. A server is not needed to launch; add sync only when a madrasa asks to see several students in one place.

09 — Two things that must be right

Get these wrong and nothing else matters

Peer listening must be free and frictionless

Students listen to one another constantly — that is where most listening actually happens, not in the teacher's hands. Support it with a simple session code, or purely local sessions with no account at all. Gate this behind institutional sign-up and the largest use case walks away.

Privacy, because these are children's voices

Recordings stay on the device by default. Explicit consent from the parent or teacher, visible retention controls, and no cloud upload unless someone deliberately turns it on. Build this in from day one — retrofitting it is painful, and a single incident would end the project.

10 — Open questions

Worth settling before code is written

  • Which printed edition is the reference — Taj Company, Muhammadi, or another 16-line Mushaf? Page breaks differ between them, and the wrong choice breaks the visual memory of every existing student.
  • Free forever, or free reader with paid teacher tools? A charitable free product reaches further; a small institutional fee funds maintenance.
  • Interface language at launch — English first, or English and Urdu together?
  • Does the first release target Zahid's son and his teacher only, or a wider beta from the start?
Recommended next step

Build a working prototype of listening mode alone — one real Mushaf page, tap-to-mark, live session timer, and the post-session review screen. Hand it to a student and a listener and watch whether the tapping actually survives a real recitation. That test will teach more than another month of planning.
